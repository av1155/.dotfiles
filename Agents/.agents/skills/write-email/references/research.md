# Research Behind the Rules

Evidence for the guidance in SKILL.md. Read this when a length or structure call needs defending, or when adapting the rules to a case they do not obviously fit.

## Length and response rate

Boomerang analysed over 40 million emails to isolate the factors that move reply rate.

| Word count | Response rate |
| --- | --- |
| 10 or fewer | 36% |
| 50 to 125 | peak band |
| 75 to 100 | 51% |
| over 200 | 44% |
| 500 to 2,000 | 44%, flat |

Two things follow. Brevity has a floor: a ten-word email reads as careless and does worse than a hundred-word one. And the penalty for length plateaus, so a 500-word email and a 2,000-word email perform about the same. Once you are past the cliff, the fix is restructuring, not shaving.

HBR reports the same shape: under 125 words at 51%, over 200 at 44%.

Caveat on transfer. This corpus is dominated by outreach and transactional mail, not internal messages between colleagues who already know each other. Treat the numbers as directional. What generalises is the mechanism: every sentence that is not doing work costs some probability of a reply.

## Reading level

Boomerang found emails written at roughly a third-grade reading level got about 36% more responses than emails written at a college level. Simpler wins over more sophisticated. This is about sentence construction and word choice, not about condescension or dumbing down the content.

## Sentiment

Messages carrying moderate positivity or moderate negativity drew 10 to 15% more responses than fully neutral ones. Heavy emotion in either direction fell back to neutral performance. Flat corporate register is a real cost.

## BLUF

Bottom Line Up Front is the US Army writing standard, codified because "the greatest weakness in ineffective writing is that it doesn't quickly transmit a focused message." The Army pairs it with an action-shaped subject tag (ACTION, DECISION, REQUEST, INFO, COORD, SIGN).

The test before writing: what am I asking this person to do as a result of reading this? That answer is the first sentence.

BLUF is strongest for internal mail and established relationships. With a stranger, or where the ask needs framing to land, one sentence of context can precede it. One.

## Inverted pyramid

Conclusion first, supporting detail second, background last. Executives scan rather than read, so a well-structured email lets the reader take the top and ignore the rest without losing anything they needed.

## One email, one ask

Each email should carry one clear request. When you truly need several, number them and make each individually scannable, so the recipient can triage and answer a subset today rather than deferring the whole thing.

This is the reasoning behind the email-plus-one-pager split: the email holds what must be acted on, the attachment holds the reference material, and the recipient replies inline against numbered lines.

## Skimmable structure

Readers scan in an F pattern. Put information-carrying words first in every heading, bullet, and paragraph opener; move keywords left. Break text with line breaks and pattern changes so nothing reads as a wall. Emphasis works only when rare.

## Reasons and outs

Attaching a reason to a request measurably raises compliance, which is why a deadline should say why it exists rather than just asserting itself. Offering an explicit way to decline lets the recipient say no without losing face, and tends to produce a faster honest answer than silence.

## Hedging and over-apologising

"Sorry to bother you" reframes a reasonable request as an interruption. The most common offenders: "just", "sorry" where nothing warrants one, "I think" before a fact, "maybe", "hopefully", "kind of", "does that make sense?"

Hedging weakens clarity and signals uncertainty the writer does not actually have. Apologising before saying anything is one of the faster ways to lose credibility in writing. The substitution that works is gratitude or nothing: "thanks for looking at this" rather than "sorry to bother you with this".

## Subject lines

5 to 7 words, at or under 50 characters, with the key information inside the first 33 so it survives truncation on mobile clients. Internal mail favours clarity over cleverness. Action words that earn their place: Action, Required, Decision, Update, FYI, New.

The framing question: why does this matter to this person today? The subject line answers that, not "what is this email about".

## Medium choice

Email is right for sharing information, documenting decisions, and driving a specific action. It is wrong for lengthy in-depth discussion and for decision-making that needs alignment.

Move rapid back-and-forth to chat. Take relationship-sensitive or difficult subjects to the highest bandwidth channel available, in person first, then video, then phone, and send email afterward to record what was agreed. A frequent failure is sending an email that should have been a meeting, and the mirror of it, calling a meeting that should have been an email.
